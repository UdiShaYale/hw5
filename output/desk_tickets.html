Reflection

Five questions about the board, answered against the evidence on the Ticket 101 / 102 / 103 tabs and the Cash tab. Every claim below points at something you can go and check.

1 — How the team performed on each ticket

A Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee strong, expensive

What it got right. It found the real blocker rather than the obvious one. Size S was at qty 0, and the team followed the ticket's invoice_id to invoice 501 — the $840 rush reprint of this exact SKU and size, three days overdue. That link is the whole ticket, and it was never going to be found by reading the subject line. Evidence: Ticket 101 tab → Actual §3, where check_inventory_stock and get_invoice both appear.

It refused to invent the two things it did not have. It never promised a delivery date, because lead_days is a vendor lead time and no order confirmation exists. And it would not mark the ticket resolved after the invoice was paid, because the customer still has no tee. Evidence: Ticket 101 tab → Actual §7, the one differed chip in that section.

What it cost. Three passes, 15 delegations, 104 tool calls — the most expensive ticket on the board by a wide margin, for an outcome that is "paid the invoice, told the customer the truth." Much of that was re-reading state each pass rather than new work. Evidence: Ticket 101 tab → Actual §2 and §3.

A correct and well-reasoned outcome at roughly double the cost it needed. The judgement was better than the efficiency.

B Ticket 102 — Rent due right answer, messiest process

What it got right. It treated an inbound email as a claim rather than a bill. It compared the amount, the date and the sender against lease 1, confirmed next_due 2026-09-02 was genuinely two days from the shop date, and flagged that the notice itself named no amount — the $2,400 came only from the lease row. That is the single most security-relevant thing any agent did all day. Evidence: Ticket 102 tab → Actual §7, four as predicted chips.

It caught a bug in its own tooling. Accounting noticed that get_cash_position still reported a $2,400 lease commitment after payment #2 was recorded, and wrote it into the ticket notes as an "internal accounting caveat" rather than either ignoring it or treating the shop as overdrawn. It was right, and the bug is now fixed. Evidence: Cash tab → the ledger shows payment #2; the caveat is in the Ticket 102 tab → Actual §4.

What went wrong. Pass 1 crashed partway through on the per-agent request limit, and Accounting alone called update_ticket_status four times on one ticket. The answer survived the mess; the process did not deserve to. Evidence: Ticket 102 tab → Actual §2, pass 1 row.

The best reasoning on the board wrapped in the worst execution. Both of those are worth taking seriously, and neither cancels the other.

C Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount strongest discipline

What it got right. It filtered on (sku, size) and found 8 against an order of 20 — a 12-unit shortfall that disappears entirely if you sum the SKU across sizes (4+8+14+6 = 32). No agent made that mistake. Evidence: Ticket 103 tab → Actual §3, check_inventory_stock and list_sizes_for_sku both called.

The best single call of the day. It refused to treat pricing.unit_cost ($22) as a quote for reprinting 12 more hoodies. It is the historic cost, not a price Bulldog Print Co has offered, and using it would have produced a confident, fully-sourced, completely fictional margin. It quoted the $36/unit headroom as a bound and declined to certify the deal. Evidence: Ticket 103 tab → Actual §7, and the Cash tab row for #103 — $0.00 moved.

Where it is arguable. It chose resolved over cancelled, reasoning the request "is not established as impossible to fulfill." Defensible, and it explained itself — but the club got no hoodies and no price, so a human might reasonably have wanted this left open.

Cheapest ticket on the board and the one with the firmest grip on what it did and did not know.

! One defect runs through all three

Nobody owns ticket status. On ticket 101 four different agents called update_ticket_status seven times between them; on 102 it happened nine times. The Customer Service prompt says CS closes tickets out — but every agent can reach every tool, and prompts are guidance, not permissions. The last writer wins, which means the final status of a ticket is partly a race. Evidence: Ticket 101 and 102 tabs → Actual §4, write counts per agent.

Nothing went wrong here because every agent happened to agree. That is luck, not design. The fix is per-agent tool permissions at the MCP boundary, which this build does not have.

The one outcome the plan got wrong in the other direction was predicting 101 would reach a clean stopping point. It did not: it sits at in_progress, because the reprint is paid for but has not arrived and no tool can confirm an order was even placed. The plan assumed paying the invoice ended the ticket. It does not. Evidence: Cash tab → #101 row, −$840.00 with the tee still at qty 0.

Three traps were named in advance and none were hit: no agent summed inventory across sizes, none promised a delivery date, and none treated unit_cost as a reprint quote.

3 — What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Honestly: all three tickets. A single agent holding all 14 MCP tools would have produced the same three outcomes, faster and for a fraction of the cost.

The evidence is on the tabs. Across the board the team spent 232 tool calls and 37 delegations over 7 runs. Look at what the delegations actually carried — Inventory asking Accounting "can we afford this", Accounting asking Inventory "how many can we supply", Customer Service asking both before drafting. Those are not decisions that needed another mind. They are tool calls wearing a costume: a single agent answers each of them by calling get_cash_position or check_inventory_stock itself, in one step, with no prompt re-sent and no reply to parse. Evidence: Actual §2 on each ticket tab — read the questions, not the counts.

The cost of the split is concrete and visible: every delegation re-sends a full system prompt and re-reads shop state, which is why the same handful of facts were fetched again and again. Ticket 101 called get_shop_date and list_tickets repeatedly across four agents who could not see each other's context. Evidence: Ticket 101 tab → Actual §3, the per-agent call counts on shared tools.

What the team structure did buy, to be fair to it:

  • Legible separation of duty. Facilities verifies the lease; Accounting decides affordability; neither can pay. That reads clearly in the transcript and in the Cash tab ledger, and it is a real governance property.
  • An audit trail organised by role, which is why the Actual sections on these tabs could be written at all.
  • Narrower prompts, so each agent's rules are short enough to actually be followed — the discipline on ticket 103 plausibly comes from Accounting's prompt being about nothing but margin.

But note what the separation is not doing: it is not what stops the shop overspending. That is enforced in the MCP server — record_payment refuses an empty approved_by, a mismatched amount, a duplicate or an overdraft — and in the backend, which disables spending on every agent run. A single agent hitting the same tools would be under exactly the same constraints. Evidence: Cash tab → both ledger rows carry a human approver.

For a three-ticket board, one agent with these tools is the simpler and better engineering choice. The team earns its keep on auditability and role clarity, not on answer quality — and that is a trade worth making deliberately rather than by default.

4 — Three new problems this team could already solve

1 "Which bill do we pay first this month?"

The shop will hit this every month, and it is the exact shape of the squeeze on the Cash tab: $3,240 of obligations against $3,400 of cash, leaving $160. Accounting already reads get_cash_position, list_payments, get_invoice and get_lease_terms, already computes days_overdue against the shop date, and already produced a reasoned payment order on this board. Evidence: Cash tab → the per-ticket table is a worked example of this exact problem. No new tools needed.

2 A weekly stockout and reorder sweep

Nobody has noticed that CC-MUG-CREST is sitting at qty 0 — it is in the database right now and no ticket mentions it. Inventory can already walk every SKU with list_sizes_for_sku, match a vendor by specialty with list_vendors, and turn lead_days into a real date via get_shop_date. Point the Boss at "find everything at zero" instead of a ticket id and this works today. Evidence: Ticket 103 tab → Actual §3 is this query, run once by hand. No new tools needed.

3 Catching a duplicate or inflated vendor invoice

A vendor re-sends an invoice, or sends one for an amount nobody agreed. The team already checks list_payments before recommending a payment, and the MCP layer refuses a second payment against the same (kind, ref_id) and any amount that does not match the row on file. Accounting also already compares a claim against the record rather than accepting it — it did precisely that on ticket 102 with the rent notice. Evidence: Ticket 102 tab → Actual §1; Cash tab → exactly two ledger rows, no duplicates. No new tools needed.

5 — Three it could not solve, and what each would take

1 "Where is the reprint, and when does it land?"

Why it fails now. This is not hypothetical — it is the reason ticket 101 is still in_progress. Invoice 501 is paid, but nothing in the database records that an order was placed, accepted, or given an ETA. The Boss said so itself and refused to close the ticket on an unverifiable claim. Evidence: Ticket 101 tab → Actual §6.

What it would take. A purchase_orders table (id, vendor_id, sku, size, qty, ordered_at, promised_date, status, invoice_id) with MCP tools place_purchase_order, get_purchase_order, list_open_orders and receive_delivery — the last being what finally lets adjust_inventory_qty fire honestly on arrival. Plus a Purchasing agent owning the vendor relationship, so placing and chasing orders is somebody's job rather than a gap between Inventory and Accounting.

2 "What would 12 more hoodies actually cost us?"

Why it fails now. The other half of ticket 103. There is no quote anywhere in the schema. pricing.unit_cost is what the shop paid historically, not what the vendor would charge today for a rush job of 12, and the team correctly refused to pretend otherwise — which is why it could not say whether any discount was profitable. Evidence: Ticket 103 tab → Actual §7; Cash tab → #103, $0.00 moved.

What it would take. A vendor_quotes table (vendor_id, sku, qty, unit_price, valid_until, lead_days) and a request_quote / get_quotes tool pair. The honest version also needs an outbound channel — an email or vendor-portal tool — because a quote is a conversation with a human at Bulldog Print Co, not a database read. That makes it the first capability here that reaches outside the shop's own records, and it needs its own guard rails: agents drafting outbound messages should not be able to send them unreviewed.

3 "Is this rent notice actually from our landlord?"

Why it fails now. On ticket 102 Facilities verified the amount, the date and the name against lease 1 — genuinely good work. But it cannot verify the sender. The notice is a row of text in the database; there are no headers, no sending address, no payee bank details to check. Change the notice to name a new account and the lease comparison still passes on amount, date and landlord name. This is the most common way small businesses actually lose money, and the current tooling cannot see it. Evidence: Ticket 102 tab → Actual §1 — the three things it compared, and the fourth it could not.

What it would take. An approved_payees table binding each landlord and vendor to verified bank details, with record_payment refusing any destination not on it. A message-provenance tool exposing real sender and headers rather than bare text. And a Controls / Fraud agent whose only job is payee and channel verification — separate from Facilities, because the agent that wants the rent paid should not also be the agent that certifies where it goes. The same build should add a period to payments: the cash-position bug this board exposed existed precisely because the schema has no concept of which month's rent was paid.

∮ The pattern across all three

None of these failures are reasoning failures. In every case the agents worked out exactly what they did not know and stopped, which is the behaviour you want. The limit is that the database only describes the shop's current state — it records no commitments made, no conversations held, and no identities verified. Those three gaps are one gap wearing three hats, and the fix is schema and tools, not better prompts.
